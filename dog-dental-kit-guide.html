Why Dog Dental Health Matters

Dogs don't get cavities the way humans do, but they do get tartar, plaque, and gum disease. Tartar buildup causes bad breath in the short term and gum disease in the long term. Gum disease is painful, progressive, and expensive to treat — professional dental cleaning under anaesthetic can cost hundreds to thousands of dollars. Regular brushing at home prevents most of this.

The other immediate benefit is breath. Dogs with clean teeth simply smell better — something any dog owner who lets their dog near their face will appreciate.

What You Need to Brush Your Dog's Teeth

  • Dog toothpaste — never use human toothpaste. It contains fluoride and xylitol which are toxic to dogs. Dog toothpaste is formulated to be safe to swallow and comes in flavours dogs actually like
  • A toothbrush — a long-handled brush lets you reach the back teeth. A finger brush gives more control for nervous dogs or difficult areas
  • Patience — most dogs need a gradual introduction. Rushing it makes the experience stressful and harder long term

How to introduce tooth brushing to your dog

The biggest mistake is going straight in with the toothbrush. Follow this gradual approach and most dogs will accept brushing within two weeks:

  • Week 1 — let your dog lick a small amount of toothpaste off your finger. Do this daily until they actively look forward to it
  • Week 2 — use your finger to rub the toothpaste gently along the outer surface of the teeth. No brush yet
  • Week 3 — introduce the finger brush. Same gentle motion, outer teeth only
  • Week 4+ — introduce the toothbrush. Start at the front teeth and gradually work back over several sessions

Always end each session with praise and a treat. The goal is for your dog to associate tooth brushing with something positive.

How often should you brush?

Daily is ideal but three times per week delivers most of the benefit. Even once a week is significantly better than never. The key is consistency — irregular brushing allows tartar to build up between sessions and reduces the overall effectiveness.

What if my dog absolutely won't tolerate brushing?

Some dogs never accept a toothbrush despite a patient introduction. Alternatives that provide some dental benefit include dental chews, water additives, and dental gels — though none are as effective as brushing. If your dog has significant existing tartar buildup, a vet cleaning may be needed before home brushing becomes effective.
